# FIRST ENCOUNTER — Hands First
## A short design note · November 16, 2026

**Two bodies, four hands, one argument.**

The first game is an encounter, not an arsenal. One combatant is controlled by a person; another is controlled by a modest reactive sparring controller. They are the same kind of actor. Either controller can occupy either body without changing its history, injury, momentum, equipment, or physical rules.

The experience flows from **choices → body and position → hands and commitment → opportunity and contact → the other combatant's hands and body → consequences → new choices**. Both sides participate. The room supplies real opportunities and limits; it does not decide moral truth or reward the player by secretly changing its rules.

## What we are making now

An enjoyable, repeatable ten-second exchange in a simple warehouse training area. Approach, raise hands, strike, defend, intercept, counter, overcommit, recover. The hands should feel expressive and available without being weightless or able to do everything at once. A missed heavy matters. A successful defensive action can become the beginning of your own combination.

Every useful pose should communicate something: readiness, reach, protection, commitment, contact, loss of balance, or recovery. Smooth motion through space; sparse, emphatic artwork. Do not impose five FPS on the controls. Do not add dozens of in-betweens to make the game feel responsive.

## What stays firm

- **One combatant, interchangeable controllers.** No special human-only physics or NPC-only counter powers.
- **Actions and consequences agree with their presentation.** The simulation resolves contact; animation makes that contact and the available next choices readable.
- **Both fighters remain interpretable.** A reactive partner may punish an observable mistake, not an input it secretly read before the player expressed it.
- **Existing work is a foundation, not a hostage.** Reuse ROOM's actor/runtime and source assets. Do not make completing another editor, the economy, or a new engine prerequisites for throwing a good punch.

## What stays loose

The controls, thresholds, move timings, amount of assistance, hand positions, and combinations are experiments. Teddy's latest scheme is **Sketch A**, not a permanent binding contract. Separate raw buttons from action intent, and make a profile easy to change. Different mappings can feel different; that is exactly why we need to test them without rewriting the fighters.

The first vocabulary is readiness, light strike, heavy strike, guard, interception, and recovery. Reach can be previewed, but the full grip/clinch/traversal vocabulary follows later. Variety should arise from timing and context, not a random animation that conceals different attack rules.

## How the larger idea survives

Punchlines will give meaningful events an answer: a gesture, a short line, an offer of help, an insult. BASED will shape delivery and interpretation; KeyWrapping will reference what actually happened and who legitimately knows about it. Momentum can become the stake of that exchange. None of this is already implemented by putting a meter on screen.

Later, the warehouse becomes the action-movie stunt set: rails, attachments, collapses, vehicles, hazards. Authored changes, convincing consequences, not expensive fracture everywhere. Ground fighting first; jumps, kicks and environmental Grab next; Punchlines next; large environmental consequences afterward. No automatic expansion during this build.

## The first proof

The player can see an attack coming, deliberately intercept it, answer with a short combination, feel the opponent's guard yield, and immediately want another exchange. Switch bodies and the same rules still work.

**The technical tests establish consistency. Teddy's play establishes whether it is fun.** We are not promising the best combat game ever; we are building the smallest exchange that gives that ambition something real to stand on.
